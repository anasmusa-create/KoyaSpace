Kepler / K2

NASA’s first planet-hunting mission. It showed the sky holds more planets than stars.

Abandoned but Not Forgotten: scroll to follow its story

Staring at one patch of sky

Launched on March 6, 2009, Kepler carried the largest digital camera sent to space up to that time. It stared at one star-filled patch of Cygnus, watching about 150,000 stars at once for Earth-sized planets.

Its main mirror was 1.4 m (4 ft 7 in) wide, one of the largest beyond Earth orbit.

How a planet shows itself

Kepler detected planets by transits: tiny dips in a star’s brightness when a planet crosses in front of it. Watch the dip in the curve as the dark planet slides across the star.

Five planets in six weeks

In its first six weeks Kepler found five exoplanets, Kepler 4b to 8b, announced in January 2010. In April 2010 it found Kepler-9, the first confirmed system with more than one planet transiting the same star, after surveying more than 156,000 stars over seven months.

Kepler-11: a crowded system

Kepler-11, a yellow dwarf about 2,000 light years away, has six planets, all larger than Earth. Kepler found that nature often packs planets close to their stars, so our own inner solar system looks sparse by comparison.

Planets are everywhere

When the mission was first imagined, 35 years earlier, no planet outside our solar system was known, said founding investigator William Borucki. The most common size Kepler found, between Earth and Neptune, does not exist in our solar system at all.

A wheel fails

In May 2013 Kepler lost the second of its four reaction wheels, the gyroscope-like parts that point the telescope. It needs three. Without them, sunlight itself, which pushes on the telescope, kept nudging Kepler off target.

K2: steering with sunlight

Engineers balanced the telescope against the pressure of sunlight, like a kayak steering into a current. The new mission, K2, scanned the sky along the ecliptic, changing fields about every three months. Planners expected 10 campaigns; it managed 19, and raised the count of surveyed stars above 500,000.

Last light

After nine years in deep space, Kepler ran out of the fuel needed for science. NASA retired it on October 30, 2018, in a safe orbit away from Earth, having found more than 2,600 planets outside our solar system.

What it left behind

Kepler’s results suggest 20 to 50 percent of the stars we see have small, possibly rocky planets in their habitable zones, where liquid water might pool. K2’s data were public immediately, and TESS, launched in April 2018, builds on Kepler’s work.
