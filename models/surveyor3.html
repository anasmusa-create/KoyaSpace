Surveyor 3

A robot that tested the Moon’s ground before astronauts dared to land on it.

Abandoned but Not Forgotten: scroll to follow its story

Is the Moon solid?

In the 1960s NASA planned to land astronauts on the Moon, but nobody was sure a heavy lander would stay on top of the surface. The Surveyor series was built to scout the ground first.

Surveyor 3 was the third engineering flight and the second in the series to make a soft landing. It carried a TV camera and, for the first time, a sampler that could reach out and dig.

Launch: April 17, 1967

An Atlas-Centaur rocket lifted off from Cape Canaveral at 07:05:01 UT. Earlier Surveyors flew straight to the Moon. This one first waited in a parking orbit around Earth, then the Centaur upper stage fired a second time to send it on its way.

The spacecraft weighed 997.9 kg (2,200 lb). Three days later it was falling toward Oceanus Procellarum.

Two bounces

On the way down, highly reflective rocks confused the lander’s radar. The engine failed to cut off at the right moment, about 4.3 m above the surface. Surveyor 3 hit the ground, bounced up, came down, bounced again, and settled at 00:04:17 UT on April 20, 1967.

Landing site: 3° south, 23.41° west, in the southeastern Oceanus Procellarum.

Its first look around

Less than an hour after landing, the TV camera began sending the first of thousands of pictures. Two lenses, 25 mm and 100 mm, sat under a mirror that could be turned horizontally and vertically. One 200-line picture took about 20 seconds to send. It could also make sharper 600-line images sent digitally.

The scoop

The most exciting experiments used the surface sampler, a scoop on an arm that reached up to 1.5 m from the lander and dug up to 18 cm deep. Following commands from Earth, it dug trenches, pressed on the ground, struck it, and moved lunar material in view of the camera.

The verdict

Scientists studied the trenches and the pictures and concluded the lunar soil had a consistency like wet sand. Its bearing strength was about 10 pounds per square inch (0.7 kg per square centimetre, or 98 kilopascals).

That was solid enough to support an Apollo Lunar Module.

The long night

The last contact with Surveyor 3 was on May 4, 1967, two days after the lunar night began. In the long, freezing dark it fell silent, with its job done.

A neighbour arrives

More than three years later, on November 18, 1969, Apollo 12 astronauts Charles Conrad, Jr. and Alan L. Bean landed the Intrepid Lunar Module about 180 m (197 yards) from the inactive Surveyor 3.

The visit

During their second moonwalk, on November 19, 1969, the astronauts walked over to Surveyor 3. They recovered parts, including the soil scoop and the camera system, so scientists could study what nearly two and a half years on the Moon’s surface had done to them.

Why it still matters

Surveyor 3 told Apollo the ground would hold. Then, thanks to the astronauts, pieces of it came home to show what long exposure on the Moon does to a machine. The rest of the lander still sits where it stopped.
