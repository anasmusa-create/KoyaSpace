Opportunity

A robot built to last three months. It worked for fourteen years.

Abandoned but Not Forgotten: scroll to follow its story

Named by a nine-year-old

In 2003, NASA ran a naming contest for two new twin rovers. The winner was Sofi Collis, age nine, who had once lived in an orphanage and found comfort looking at the night sky. She wrote about the spirit and the opportunity that America had given her. The rovers became Spirit and Opportunity.

Opportunity left Earth on a Delta II rocket on July 7, 2003, folded inside a protective capsule. It spent about seven months crossing space to reach Mars.

Six minutes, then a bounce

On January 25, 2004 (UTC), the capsule hit Mars’ thin air at roughly 19,000 km/h. A heat shield burned off speed, a parachute opened, rockets fired, and airbags inflated around the rover. Then it dropped and bounced across the plain more than twenty times.

It rolled to a stop inside a small crater, which the team named Eagle. They called it a hole in one: bedrock was exposed right at the rover’s wheels.

Rocks that remember water

This was the first bedrock outcrop a rover had ever studied on Mars. Opportunity reached out with its arm. Its Mössbauer spectrometer found jarosite, a mineral that forms in acidic water. Its microscope showed ripples frozen in the layers, the kind made by shallow, moving water.

The tiny grey spheres in the soil, nicknamed “blueberries”, were hematite grown inside wet rock. In March 2004 NASA announced it: Meridiani Planum was once soaked in salty water.

A stranger on the plain

In January 2005, Opportunity drove to a dark lump of metal that did not belong. It sat near the rover’s own discarded heat shield, so the team called it Heat Shield Rock.

It was an iron-nickel meteorite, the first ever identified on another planet. A visitor from the asteroid belt had been lying on Mars all along, waiting for someone to look.

Stuck in the sand

On April 26, 2005, Opportunity drove onto a soft ripple of sand called Purgatory Dune and sank up to its axles. Its wheels spun and went nowhere.

Engineers copied the dune in a sandbox at JPL and practised on a test rover. For about five weeks they backed Opportunity out a few centimetres at a time. On June 4 it rolled free, and the team learned to avoid soft sand ripples.

Victoria, a crater with cliffs

Victoria crater is about 730 metres wide, more than seven football fields. Opportunity arrived in 2006 and spent nearly two years exploring its rim, photographing layered cliffs like the pages of a history book.

Each layer was a different chapter of Martian weather and water. In 2008 it left, and set a new goal: Endeavour crater, far across the plain.

Endeavour, and a marathon

The drive to Endeavour took almost three years. At the rim in 2011, Opportunity found a bright vein of gypsum named Homestake, a mineral left by water flowing through rock. In 2015 it found clay minerals, which form in gentler water than the acid of its first site.

In March 2015 it passed the distance of a marathon, 42.195 km.

Older, slower, still working

Planned for 90 sols, it worked for 5,111. It aged like any machine. A front wheel motor wore down, so it sometimes drove backward. In 2014 its flash memory began to fail, and engineers worked around it. Dust built up on its panels, until a wind gust cleaned them and the power jumped back.

The storm

In late May 2018, a dust storm began near Opportunity and grew until it wrapped the whole planet. By early June the sky above the rover was darker than any it had ever seen.

Solar power fell almost to zero. The rover put itself into a low-power sleep, waking now and then to check. Earth waited and listened.

June 10, 2018

That was the last time Opportunity spoke. It was resting in Perseverance Valley, on the rim of Endeavour. When the storm cleared in autumn, NASA sent more than a thousand recovery commands. Nothing replied.

On February 13, 2019, NASA declared the mission complete, after 5,111 sols on Mars.

A news reporter once translated its final data as “My battery is low and it’s getting dark.” That is a poetic paraphrase, not words the rover wrote.

Why it still matters

Opportunity proved a lot of Mars once had liquid water, and that told later missions where to look. Curiosity and Perseverance followed its path, one question at a time. The rover stays on Mars, but its discoveries are still being used.
